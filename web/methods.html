Methods and caveats

This tool compares US counties and census tracts on property tax, rent, prices and how much housing gets built. It is a screening aid: it narrows a long list of places to a short one. It does not replace checking the actual tax bill, rent roll and zoning for a specific property.

What the tax figures mean

Every tax figure here comes from the American Community Survey, which asks owner-occupiers what they paid in real estate taxes last year. That makes it a measure of what current residents pay, not of what a new owner or a landlord would pay.

The gap differs from state to state, because the law treats owners differently:

  • Assessment caps. Some states limit how fast a home's taxable value can rise while the owner stays put, and reset it at sale. A buyer then pays tax on the purchase price while neighbours pay on a much older value.
  • Homestead exemptions. Many states cut the bill for a primary residence. A rental or second home does not qualify.
  • Property classes. Some states tax rental, commercial and owner-occupied property at different rates. South Carolina, for example, assesses an owner-occupied home at 4% of its value and a rental at 6%, and exempts only the owner-occupied home from school operating taxes.

So the rate shown is not just too low for an investor by a fixed amount. In some states a rental pays about the same as an owner-occupier; in others it pays two to three times as much. A ranking by this rate partly ranks states by how generously they treat owner-occupiers. Look up the local assessor's rules for a non-homestead property before relying on it.

A low tax rate is also not a better deal in itself. Buyers pay more for a home with a low tax bill, so much of the difference is already in the price. What matters is the rate a new owner will face, whether it is likely to change, and what the tax pays for.

Measures

MeasureHow it is calculated
Owner-occupier tax rate Total real estate taxes paid by owner-occupiers ÷ total value of their homes.
Owner-occupier tax bill Total real estate taxes paid ÷ number of owner-occupied homes.
Gross rent yield 12 × median contract rent ÷ median home value.
Gross yield minus owner tax rate Gross rent yield minus the owner-occupier tax rate.
Rental vacancy rate Vacant homes for rent ÷ (rented homes + vacant homes for rent + homes rented but not yet occupied).
Price-to-income ratio Median home value ÷ median income of all households.
Rent as share of income 12 × median gross rent ÷ median income of all households.
Growth after inflation Total change between the first and latest survey periods, adjusted with the national CPI-U. A 12-year total, not an annual rate.
Homes permitted per 1,000 existing Average homes authorised by building permit per year over the latest five years ÷ existing homes × 1,000.
Share of permits in 5+ unit buildings Permitted homes in buildings of five or more ÷ all permitted homes, same years.
Percentile The share of counties, nationally or in the same state, with a lower value. State percentiles are not shown for states with fewer than five counties.

Things to keep in mind

  • High yield mostly marks cheap markets. Rents cannot fall far below the cost of running a building, while prices in a declining place can. So the highest yields are concentrated in places that are losing people and have high poverty and vacancy. Read yield next to population growth and rental vacancy, not on its own.
  • Yield is a rough proxy. It divides the rent of rented homes by the value of owned homes, which are different buildings. It ignores vacancy, maintenance, insurance and financing. "Gross yield minus owner tax rate" removes one cost only and is not a net yield.
  • Every figure is a five-year average. "2020–24" means survey responses from 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars. Home values and rents rose quickly in those years, so the figures sit below today's prices.
  • The starting period includes the housing bust. Growth is measured from 2008–12. Places where prices fell hardest then show the largest gains, and part of that is recovery.
  • Growth mixes appreciation with new building. A rising median home value can mean existing homes gained value, or that newer, dearer homes were added.
  • Adjacent periods share a year. The four periods are 2008–12, 2012–16, 2016–20 and 2020–24. Each shares its first year with the one before, so neighbouring points on a trend chart are not fully independent.
  • How older home values are adjusted. Before its 2018–22 release the Census did not adjust home values for inflation within a five-year period, as it did for rent and income. Older home values are scaled here by the ratio of the final year's CPI to the period's average CPI so that all figures are in final-year dollars.
  • Why the tax rate uses totals, not medians. The Census caps the published median tax at $10,001 and the median home value at $2,000,001. In the most expensive places the medians sit on those caps, so a rate built from them is wrong. Totals are not capped.
  • Capped values are marked. Where a median home value or rent is at a Census cap, measures that use it carry a ⚠. Where it is at the Census floor ($9,999 for value, $99 for rent), ratios built on it are left blank.
  • Small samples are marked. A ⚠ also appears where the margin of error on home value, rent or tax implies a coefficient of variation above 30%, where a home value or rent median is published without a margin of error, or where fewer than 100 rented or 100 owned homes stand behind the figures. This is common for tracts and small rural counties, and tract yields in particular should be read as rough.
  • No trend for tracts. Tract boundaries were redrawn in 2020, so an older tract with the same code can cover different land. Tracts show the latest period only.
  • No trend where county boundaries changed. Connecticut replaced its eight counties with nine planning regions in 2022. Bedford County, Virginia, absorbed the city of Bedford in 2013, and five areas in Alaska were redrawn or split. Growth measures are blank for all of these.
  • Permits are not zoning. Permit counts show where housing is being built, which follows demand at least as much as rules: most places with few permits are places few people are moving to. For a landlord, many permits also mean competing supply. The counts include estimates for permit offices that did not report. About 3% of counties have no permit data, and 27 counties, including all of Connecticut, have fewer than five years of it; those are marked.
  • Missing figures and filters. A county with no figure for a measure is left out when you filter on that measure.
  • Puerto Rico is not included.
